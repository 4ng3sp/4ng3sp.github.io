Netflix: Network Science & Text Mining

A group project exploring collaboration networks and the language of the Netflix USA catalogue across three time windows.

Problem / Objective

Explore how the relational structure and narrative content of the Netflix USA catalogue differ across 2010–2015, 2016–2020 and 2021–2025. The project combines networks of people and genres with the analysis of titles and plot descriptions.

Data

Catalogue records include cast, directors, genres, titles, descriptions, dates and popularity-related fields. Networks are built separately for three time windows. In the actor–actor network, an edge indicates that two actors appeared in the same film. The report also studies actor–director, actor–genre and director–genre networks. A verified dataset URL and full acquisition history are not supplied with this portfolio.

Methods

The group computes degree, betweenness, harmonic and eigenvector centrality, PageRank and global graph summaries. Community comparisons use Louvain, Leiden, Walktrap and Infomap. Text processing includes lowercasing, tokenisation, stopword removal, word and bigram frequencies, TF-IDF, LDA topic modelling and cosine similarity. Bing-lexicon sentiment analysis characterises the language of plot descriptions. Semantic network measures are also compared with popularity-related variables.

Results

The reported actor–actor networks contain 6,128, 5,238 and 4,938 nodes across the three periods, with 21,239, 16,085 and 13,930 edges. Their clustering coefficients rise from 0.793 to 0.821 and 0.849, indicating increasingly cohesive local groups within the analysed snapshots. Actor–director Louvain modularity is reported as 0.768, 0.833 and 0.870. Text analysis identifies genre-specific vocabulary and recurring topics. Negative lexical sentiment is common in crime, war, horror and conflict-oriented descriptions; this describes plot language, not viewer satisfaction. The report finds that semantic centrality is not interchangeable with popularity.

Limitations

The catalogue is an observational, selected collection rather than the full film industry. Differences between periods can reflect changes in coverage, window length or sample composition. Dense cast cliques can generate high clustering mechanically. Disconnected graphs require care when interpreting distance-based centrality. LDA topics and lexicon sentiment depend on preprocessing and interpretation. Some repeated tables and narrative values are inconsistent; this page uses the cited network tables and avoids stronger causal or industry-wide claims.

My contribution

For the actor–actor collaboration networks, I developed the analysis code, interpreted the results and wrote the chapter. I also developed the code for text mining of film titles and plot descriptions, interpreted the findings and wrote the corresponding chapter. The full report and the broader results presented on this page are shared team outputs.
